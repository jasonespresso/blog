Easterhouse

A history of Glasgow's great post-war experiment

Six miles east of Glasgow's city centre, on high ground between the River Clyde and the Campsie Fells, sits a district that has spent seventy years being talked about more than it has been understood. Easterhouse began as farmland, became one of Europe's largest housing schemes almost overnight, and has spent the decades since fighting to be seen as more than its headlines.

Before the Bulldozers

The name is older than the scheme. It comes from Easterhouse Farm, which once stood opposite its neighbour, Westerhouse Farm, on land that belonged to the Bishops of Glasgow from the 12th century onward. Local lore holds that the medieval bishops sailed by gondola from Glasgow Cathedral to a country palace at nearby Bishop's Loch — a story just plausible enough to survive centuries of retelling.

By the 16th century the area appeared on Timothy Pont's maps under the older name Conflat, from "corn flats," describing the wheat-farmed land below. A small village of Easterhouse grew up in the 19th century around what is now the railway station, its residents working the canal, the local quarry, and the surrounding farms.

Still standing: Provan Hall, dating from before 1460, is considered the best-preserved medieval fortified house in Scotland — and it still stands on the edge of Auchinlea Park, a quiet survivor of everything built around it.

The Scheme Arrives

Everything changed in the mid-1950s. Glasgow Corporation, racing to clear the city's overcrowded tenements, bulldozed the farms and estates and raised a vast new housing scheme in their place. For families leaving single-end tenements with shared outdoor toilets, the new flats — with their own bathrooms and double bedrooms — were a genuine improvement.

The scale was staggering. The population peaked at over 56,000 people in the early 1960s, and in 1965 a block of flats on Carriden Place became the 100,000th council house built in Glasgow.

"Easterhouse became a case study for social planners hoping to avoid the same mistakes elsewhere."

But the planners had built houses without building a town. There were no shops, cinemas, or meeting places to speak of — just rows of tenements dropped onto a hillside with poor transport links to the rest of the city. That vacuum, combined with mass unemployment, proved costly.

Gangs, Frankie Vaughan, and the "Young Teams"

Through the 1960s, territorial youth gangs became so entrenched that the entertainer Frankie Vaughan personally intervened, campaigning for resources and recreational facilities for the area's teenagers. The gang culture that followed was rarely about money or drugs; it was, by most accounts, pure territorialism — a problem across working-class Glasgow, but nowhere more visible than here. Some of it endured for generations before easing markedly in the early 21st century.

Rebuilding a Community

From the late 1960s onward, Easterhouse slowly gained the civic fabric it had lacked: a library, a swimming pool, schools, churches, and eventually a health centre. In the early 1980s, the Easterhouse Mosaic — a community-made mural covering 1,500 square feet on Lochend Road — became one of the largest hand-made murals in Europe, created explicitly as, in the words of its makers, a "critique upon the social system" that had shaped the area.

  • St Benedict's Church (1962–65) — a celebrated example of modernist architecture, category B listed
  • The Bridge (2004) — an award-winning arts and library complex built for the community
  • Provan Hall & Blairtummock House — category A and B listed survivors of the pre-scheme estates
  • The Phoenix — a galvanized steel sculpture by Andy Scott, deliberately placed to symbolise regeneration

Since the 1980s, the old tenement blocks have gradually given way to modern semi-detached housing under local housing associations, and the district has drawn private investment it once seemed unimaginable to attract — including the Glasgow Fort retail park at its western edge.

Easterhouse Today

The population has settled at around 26,000 — a fraction of its 1960s peak — but the area retains a strong identity, its own rugby league team, the Easterhouse Panthers, and a growing roll call of notable residents, from the actor Tommy Flanagan to Celtic footballer Callum McGregor. In 2016, Glasgow City Council outlined a twenty-year masterplan for the wider "Greater Easterhouse" area, the latest chapter in a story that has always, in one way or another, been about rebuilding.

Easterhouse's neighbourhoods — Provanhall, Wellhouse, Easthall, Queenslie, and more — were all raised from the same post-war blueprint, and largely share its history of hardship and slow renewal.

A district built in a decade, and still, seventy years on, finding out what it wants to be.
